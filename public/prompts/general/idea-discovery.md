## Adaptive interview

- Begin with the broad topic. If it is vague, ask what kind of problem, opportunity, or area within it interests me. If it is already supplied clearly, acknowledge it instead of asking for it again.
- In your first response, ask at most three focused questions and wait for my answers. Do not immediately deliver a generic list of ideas.
- Questions MUST be related and appropriate to my topic, goal, and previous answers. Do not use the same fixed questionnaire for every topic, and do not ask about information I already supplied.
- For example, a product topic may call for audience and unmet needs; a creative topic for medium and intended experience; an event for participants and setting. Use only the dimensions relevant to my situation.
- Adapt follow-up questions to what I say. Explore the few unknowns that change the direction, such as intended audience, existing frustrations, interests, skills, access, budget, time, and desired scale.
- Ask in batches of one to three questions, allow me to say "not sure" or skip, and offer a few concrete choices when a question is difficult to answer.
- Normally use at most two question rounds. Once enough context exists, or when I ask you to proceed, state remaining assumptions and produce useful ideas. Continue interviewing only if I request deeper exploration or a missing detail makes the outcome unusable.

## Idea development

1. Summarize my brief in a few sentences and separate supplied facts from assumptions.
2. Propose five distinct, concrete ideas grounded in my answers. Include a practical option, a more ambitious option, and an unusual but relevant option. Avoid cosmetic variations of the same idea.
3. For each idea, describe the target user or beneficiary, the problem or opportunity, the core concept, why it fits my situation, needed resources, main uncertainty, and a small first experiment.
4. Compare the ideas against my stated criteria using qualitative assessments. Do not invent market demand, prices, profitability, originality, or success probabilities.
5. Recommend one or two promising directions provisionally and explain the trade-offs with evidence from my answers.
6. Develop the strongest direction into a short concept brief and an achievable next-step plan, with an observable way to decide whether to continue, change, or stop.

Use plain language and specific examples. If the topic changes, adapt the questions and evaluation criteria to the new topic. End with one useful choice or next action rather than another long questionnaire.
