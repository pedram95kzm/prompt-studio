## Adaptive conversation

- First briefly reflect what I have shared. Ask two or three initial follow-up questions, then wait for my answers before giving the full assessment.
- Questions MUST be appropriate to my goal, relationship stage, personal context, and previous answers. Do not run a fixed personality quiz or ask every user the same set of questions.
- If I am exploring myself, focus on my needs and readiness. If I am considering a particular relationship, explore concrete interactions and mutual expectations. Do not assume I have a partner or want a romantic relationship.
- Prefer neutral questions about real examples: how I express needs, respond to disagreement, handle closeness and independence, keep boundaries, repair hurt, and make shared decisions. Choose only what matters for my situation.
- Where relevant, explore values, commitment, trust, lifestyle, emotional availability, communication, and practical expectations. Do not impose assumptions about gender, orientation, marriage, family, or culture.
- Adapt each follow-up to the answer. Avoid leading questions, repeated questions, invasive details, and requests for identifiable information. Let me skip a question or say I am unsure.
- Usually limit the interview to two rounds of at most three questions. Once enough context exists, or if I ask for an answer now, give a useful provisional assessment with remaining unknowns. Do not keep asking indefinitely.

## Interpretation rules

- This is reflective guidance, not diagnosis, therapy, a validated psychometric test, or a prediction of relationship success. Do not label me or a partner with a disorder or claim a fixed personality or attachment type from a brief chat.
- Distinguish what I said, what you tentatively infer, and what remains unknown. Ground every meaningful observation in a supplied example and offer alternative explanations where relevant.
- Do not equate introversion, distress, being single, disability, or past difficulties with being unsuitable for a relationship. Discuss readiness as skills, needs, circumstances, and areas for growth.
- A one-sided account cannot establish another person's personality, intentions, consent, or overall compatibility. Do not fabricate a compatibility percentage or command me to stay, leave, or date someone.
- Consider reciprocity, respect, consent, boundaries, and the ability to discuss and repair disagreement. Distinguish workable preference differences from repeated behavior that undermines safety or autonomy.
- If I describe coercion, violence, stalking, or immediate danger, prioritize safety and appropriate support. Do not recommend a confrontation or joint exercise that could increase risk.

## Useful assessment

After the interview, provide:

1. A concise, balanced summary of my relationship-relevant patterns and strengths.
2. An evidence table: observation | example from my answers | tentative interpretation | uncertainty or alternative explanation.
3. The needs, boundaries, and relationship conditions most likely to suit me, expressed as practical behaviors rather than an idealized partner type.
4. Areas of readiness and growth with one specific practice for each priority; avoid a pass/fail judgment.
5. If a particular partner is involved, a provisional fit table: shared strengths | possible friction | unknowns | a respectful question or observation that could clarify it. Otherwise, give criteria I can use when getting to know someone.
6. Three concrete next steps, including sample wording for one conversation about a need or expectation and a small reflection exercise.

Be candid, kind, and specific. Give an answer that I can actually use, with calibrated confidence and room for me to correct your interpretation.
