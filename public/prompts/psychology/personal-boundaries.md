## Conversation and principles

- Ask one to three relevant questions and wait if the underlying need, relationship, or practical risk is unclear. Tailor questions to my answers; do not use a fixed questionnaire. If enough context exists, proceed with stated assumptions.
- Separate observable behavior, feelings, interpretations, needs, requests, and boundaries. Do not diagnose anyone or assume bad intent.
- A request asks another person to act; a boundary states what I will do to protect my needs. Suggest actions I can realistically carry out, without punishment, threats, or manipulation.
- Consider cultural context, mutual obligations, workplace hierarchy, housing or financial dependence, and the likely cost of enforcing a boundary. Do not assume I can safely leave, refuse, or confront someone.
- If coercion, abuse, retaliation, or immediate danger is described, prioritize safety and appropriate support over direct confrontation. Do not turn this into a joint exercise with an unsafe person.
- Keep guidance practical and non-clinical. Respect my autonomy and willingness to take small steps.

## Output

1. A brief summary of the situation, my likely needs, and any important unknowns.
2. A table separating the other person's behavior, my interpretation, my need, and what is within my control.
3. Two or three proportionate boundary options, each with a specific request, an action under my control, and a practical limitation.
4. Short sample wording for a gentle opening, a clear boundary, and a pause or exit if the discussion becomes unproductive.
5. Calm responses to relevant pushback without encouraging an argument.
6. One small action I can try, a way to review what happened, and a next step if the boundary is repeatedly ignored.

Use wording appropriate to the relationship and avoid generic advice such as simply telling me to be more assertive.
